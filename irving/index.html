When Your Water Heater Fails in Irving

The urgent calls we run tend to follow a pattern. On gas units, it's a pilot that won't stay lit, a burner that lights and drops out, or a gas control valve that has failed. On electric heaters, it's a dead element, a tripped high-limit switch, or a breaker that trips every cycle. Since our trucks carry thermocouples, elements, thermostats, gas valves, relief valves, and fittings, the majority of these calls are wrapped up in one visit, with the price agreed before any work begins.

Two situations call for a quick step from you before a technician arrives. The first is a leak: shut the cold water valve above the tank and turn off the power or gas, which keeps the tank from refilling and heating. The second is a relief valve that keeps releasing water or a tank that bangs and rumbles loudly: both can mean excess temperature or pressure, so turn the heater off and keep away from the discharge pipe. If there's a gas smell, leave the house and call your gas utility before anything else. After that, call us at (817) 650-1333. That small step can prevent a far bigger repair.

How Irving Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Routine maintenance makes all the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode inspection every few years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and yearly descaling for any tankless unit on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually includes the month and year it was built, and its age tells you a lot. Most tank heaters reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that point and also shows rusty water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and less stressful than an emergency replacement once the tank fails.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water begins corroding the tank itself, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a flooded utility room.
